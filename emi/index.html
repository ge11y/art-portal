The story

Emi means spirit.

In Yoruba thought it is also breath, the self, the spark Olódùmarè places in a body so clay can live. When the body is finished, emi does not end. It goes home. Sometimes it comes back.

Ogbe already knew the other half of that story. His earlier work, Eegun, belonged to the ancestors who return in cloth and mask: the visible dead, the ones who walk the market and speak in the voice of a frog under water. Emi by Ogbe is what remains after the cloth is folded away. Not the masquerade. The breath inside it.

The story the drawings keep telling is simple.

There were two hundred and twenty-two breaths that would not stay buried.

They had been people once. Some were loud. Some were almost nothing. One had a laugh like a snapped string. One had never been named. After the last drum faded, they did not become silence. They became shapes that wanted outlines.

Ogbe sat with a black line and a field of acid lime, the color of something still electric after the body is gone.

He did not invent them. He caught them.

Each spirit arrived differently. One came as a long white throat and a skull that looked surprised to still have eyes. One came as a halo, a lightning shard, a single staring circle, a body wrapped like a banner in wind. Some were almost toys. Some were almost weapons. All of them were 1/1, because a spirit is not a trait sheet. A spirit is a particular life insisting on form.

He drew them by hand so the line would shake a little, the way breath shakes. Two hundred and twenty-two times he did this, until the last one had a body it could keep.

Then he set them on Robinhood Chain, a ledger built for things that used to live only in the old world of paper and memory. The joke, if there is one, is gentle: the market never closes, and neither does a soul that has been given an outline.

Collectors do not own the dead. They keep a small, bright window open so the emi can look out. When someone puts one of these drawings in a wallet, the old arrangement is completed in a new place.

The ancestor had the mask. The living have the chain. The spirit has the drawing.

Ogbe’s name is not an accident either. In Ifá, Ogbe is the first odù, the opening, the light that starts the counting of the world. The artist who signs Ogbe in the corner of a neon field is doing what that name already knows how to do: begin.

So the collection is not a set of characters. It is a census of breaths that refused to stay invisible.

Two hundred and twenty-two spirits. One hand. A color so loud the afterlife cannot sleep through it.

That is the art. The rest is just the door they walked through.

Words by OgBe. OgBe Meji is a multidisciplinary artist and musician in San Juan, Puerto Rico, and the drop was announced here. More of his work at ogbe.art.
